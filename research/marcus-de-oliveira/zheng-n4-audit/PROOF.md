# The Marcus–de Oliveira conjecture for $n=4$: the assembled proof and the status of every step

**Theorem (Zheng, 2026).** Let $A,B\in\mathbb C^{4\times4}$ be normal matrices with eigenvalues $a_1,\ldots,a_4$ and $b_1,\ldots,b_4$, counted with multiplicity. Then

$$\det(A+B)\in\operatorname{conv}\Bigl\{z_\sigma=\prod_{i=1}^{4}(a_i+b_{\sigma(i)}):\sigma\in S_4\Bigr\}.$$

The proof is due to Dongzhe (Denzel) Zheng: *The Marcus–de Oliveira conjecture for n = 4*, 24 October 2026, [doi:10.13140/RG.2.2.36790.25923](https://doi.org/10.13140/RG.2.2.36790.25923), with a public supplement of 208 proof documents. This file assembles that proof in the order of its logical dependencies and records, for each step, where it is proved and how far it has been checked. Sections 1 to 6 and 10 contain complete proofs of the general argument. Sections 7 to 9 state the tools and the ninety cases, with a pointer to the proof of each. Where the public material leaves a step unproved, the proof supplied by this audit is cited.

## 1. From normal matrices to one unitary matrix

**1.1 (proved here).** Write $A=V_1D_aV_1^*$ and $B=V_2D_bV_2^*$ with $V_1,V_2$ unitary, $D_a=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_4)$ and $D_b=\operatorname{diag}(b_1,\ldots,b_4)$. With $U=V_1^*V_2$,

$$\det(A+B)=\det(D_a+UD_bU^*).$$

So the theorem says that $\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)$ lies in the hull of the $z_\sigma$ for every $U\in\mathrm U(4)$ and all $a,b\in\mathbb C^4$. For a permutation matrix $U=P_\sigma$, with a 1 in row $i$ and column $\sigma(i)$, the determinant equals $z_\sigma$.

**1.2 Minor expansion (proved here).** For every $U\in\mathrm U(4)$,

$$\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)=\sum_{I,J\subset[4],\ |I|=|J|}\ \prod_{i\notin I}a_i\prod_{j\in J}b_j\ \lvert\det U[I,J]\rvert^2,$$

where $U[I,J]$ is the submatrix with rows $I$ and columns $J$ and the empty minor is 1.

*Proof.* For any matrix $M$, $\det(D_a+M)=\sum_I\prod_{i\notin I}a_i\det M[I,I]$, by multilinearity of the determinant in the rows. With $M=UD_bU^*$, the Cauchy–Binet formula gives $\det M[I,I]=\sum_{|J|=|I|}\det U[I,J]\prod_{j\in J}b_j\,\overline{\det U[I,J]}$. $\square$

The determinant therefore depends on $U$ only through the vector $r(U)=(\lvert\det U[I,J]\rvert^2)_{I,J}$, and it is a fixed linear function of that vector.

## 2. Step 1: from unitary to real orthogonal matrices

**Theorem 2.1 (Zheng, Theorem 1.2).** For every $U\in\mathrm U(4)$ there are $O_-,O_+\in\mathrm O(4)$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$ with $r(U)=\lambda\,r(O_-)+(1-\lambda)\,r(O_+)$.

Three proofs are on record.

**Corollary 2.2 (proved here).** Fix $a,b\in\mathbb C^4$. If $\det(D_a+OD_bO^t)$ lies in the hull of the $z_\sigma$ for every $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$, then so does $\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)$ for every $U\in\mathrm U(4)$.

*Proof.* By 1.2 and Theorem 2.1, $\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)=\lambda\det(D_a+O_-D_bO_-^t)+(1-\lambda)\det(D_a+O_+D_bO_+^t)$. If $\det O=-1$, put $S=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$. Then $OS\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$ and $(OS)D_b(OS)^t=OD_bO^t$. So both determinants on the right lie in the hull, and the hull is convex. $\square$

## 3. The matching cone

**3.1 Matching invariants.** For eight vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_8\in\mathbb C^2$ write $[ij]=\det(v_i,v_j)$. A perfect matching of $\{1,\ldots,8\}$ gives the product of its four brackets. The fourteen noncrossing matchings

$$\begin{aligned}
&B_0=[12][34][56][78],\ B_1=[12][34][58][67],\ B_2=[12][36][45][78],\ B_3=[12][38][45][67],\ B_4=[12][38][47][56],\\
&B_5=[14][23][56][78],\ B_6=[14][23][58][67],\ B_7=[16][23][45][78],\ B_8=[16][25][34][78],\ B_9=[18][23][45][67],\\
&B_{10}=[18][23][47][56],\ B_{11}=[18][25][34][67],\ B_{12}=[18][27][34][56],\ B_{13}=[18][27][36][45]
\end{aligned}$$

span all matching products, by repeated use of $[ij][kl]-[ik][jl]+[il][jk]=0$. Put $\Psi(v)=(B_0(v),\ldots,B_{13}(v))\in\mathbb C^{14}=V_\mathbb C$ and $V_\mathbb R=\mathbb R^{14}$.

**3.2 The matrix $P$ (audit, EXACT).** For $\sigma\in S_4$ let $p_\sigma=\prod_i[i,4+\sigma(i)]$. There is an integer $24\times14$ matrix $P$ with $p_\sigma=\sum_aP_{\sigma a}B_a$ identically. It has rank 14, so $P$ is injective, and its last column is all ones: with $\kappa=e_{13}$, $P\kappa=(1,\ldots,1)$. For $x\in V_\mathbb C$ write $F_\sigma(x)=(Px)_\sigma$. Two independent implementations agree on $P$: [`lib/moc4lib.py`](lib/moc4lib.py) and [`reduction/check_section3.py`](reduction/check_section3.py).

**3.3 The cone $X$.** Let $X$ be the Zariski closure of $\Psi((\mathbb C^2)^8)$.

- $X$ is an irreducible cone. *Proved here:* it is the closure of the image of an irreducible variety, and scaling one column by $\theta$ multiplies $\Psi$ by $\theta$.
- $\dim X=6$. *Audit, EXACT:* the differential of $\Psi$ has rank 6 at a rational point, and ten independent vector fields (three from $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, seven from the torus $\{\prod t_i=1\}$) lie in its kernel everywhere.
- **Finite spectra (cited).** Every nonzero $x\in X$ equals $\alpha\,\Psi(v)$ with $v_i=(a_i,1)$, $v_{4+j}=(-b_j,1)$, finite $a,b\in\mathbb C^4$ and $\alpha\neq0$. Then $[i,4+j]=a_i+b_j$ and
  $$F_\sigma(x)=\alpha\prod_i(a_i+b_{\sigma(i)})=\alpha z_\sigma.$$
  This is the closedness of the image of $\Psi$: surjectivity of the quotient map for the reductive group $\mathrm{SL}_2\times\{\prod t_i=1\}$ (Mumford–Fogarty–Kirwan, *Geometric Invariant Theory*, Theorem 1.1) together with generation of the invariant ring in degree one (Kempe; see Howard–Millson–Snowden–Vakil, *Proc. LMS* 105 (2012), §2.1). The elementary parts are proved in [`reduction/contact-reduction.md`](reduction/contact-reduction.md), §2a: a configuration with five or more coincident points maps to 0, any other configuration maps to a nonzero vector, and a change of affine chart makes all eight points finite at the cost of one common factor.
- $\kappa\in X$: take $v_1=\cdots=v_4=(0,1)$ and $v_5=\cdots=v_8=(1,1)$.

**3.4 The cubic (audit, EXACT).** Let

$$\begin{aligned}C(m)={}&m_{13}(m_0m_6-m_1m_5)-m_0m_9(m_0+m_1+m_2+m_3+m_5+m_7+m_9)-m_1m_5m_9\\
&+m_{10}(-m_0m_3-m_0m_9+m_1m_2+m_1m_7)+m_{11}(-m_0m_7-m_0m_9+m_2m_5+m_3m_5)\\
&+m_{12}(-m_0m_9+m_1m_7-m_2m_6-m_2m_9+m_3m_5+m_3m_7)+m_2m_{10}m_{11}-m_3m_8m_{10}-m_4m_7m_{11}+m_4m_8m_9.\end{aligned}$$

Then $\nabla C$ vanishes identically on $\Psi((\mathbb C^2)^8)$, so $X\subset\{\nabla C=0\}$. Also $\nabla C(\kappa)=0$, and $H_\kappa=\operatorname{Hess}C(\kappa)=e_0e_6^t+e_6e_0^t-e_1e_5^t-e_5e_1^t$ has rank 4 and kernel $E=\{m_0=m_1=m_5=m_6=0\}$. Since $C$ is a cubic, $\nabla C(x+t\kappa)=\nabla C(x)+tH_\kappa x$. Only the inclusion $X\subset\{\nabla C=0\}$ is used. The equality cited in the paper is not needed.

**3.5 The entry subspace (proved here, given 3.4).** For a real $4\times4$ matrix $Q=(q_{ij})$ there is a unique $E(Q)\in V_\mathbb R$ with $(PE(Q))_\sigma=\sum_iq_{i,\sigma(i)}$. These vectors form $E\cap V_\mathbb R$, of dimension 10 (EXACT).

**3.6 Gaps, contacts, Leibniz sums.** Fix a reference permutation $\pi_*$. For $x\in V_\mathbb C$ put

$$g_\sigma(x)=F_\sigma(x)-F_{\pi_*}(x),\qquad c(x)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(x-F_{\pi_*}(x)\kappa\bigr)\in V_\mathbb R,\qquad (Pc(x))_\sigma=\operatorname{Re}g_\sigma(x).$$

The numbers $\operatorname{Re}g_\sigma(x)$ are the *gaps*. When all gaps are $\geq0$, the permutations with gap 0 are the *contact permutations* and form the *contact set*. Replacing $\pi_*$ by another contact permutation does not change $c(x)$. By injectivity of $P$, the common kernel of the $g_\sigma$ is $\mathbb C\kappa$.

For $c\in V_\mathbb R$ and $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$ the *Leibniz sum* is

$$\Phi_c(O)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_4}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,(Pc)_\sigma\prod_iO_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$

**3.7 (proved here).** $\Phi_\kappa(O)=\det O=1$. If $x=\alpha\Psi(v(a,b))$, then

$$\Phi_{c(x)}(O)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\alpha\det(D_a+OD_bO^t)-F_{\pi_*}(x)\bigr).$$

*Proof.* $O$ is real, so $\Phi_{c(x)}(O)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\alpha\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\prod_iO_{i,\sigma(i)}(a_i+b_{\sigma(i)})\bigr)-\operatorname{Re}F_{\pi_*}(x)\det O$. The inner sum is $\det(D_aO+OD_b)=\det(D_a+OD_bO^t)\det O$. $\square$

**3.8 (proved here).** For real $Q$ and $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$, $\Phi_{E(Q)}(O)=\sum_{i,j}q_{ij}O_{ij}^2$. Consequently, if $PE(Q)\geq0$ then $\Phi_{E(Q)}(O)\geq0$.

*Proof.* $\Phi_{E(Q)}(O)=\sum_{i,j}q_{ij}O_{ij}\operatorname{cof}_{ij}(O)$, and $\operatorname{cof}(O)=\det(O)\,O^{-t}=O$. The matrix $(O_{ij}^2)$ is doubly stochastic. By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem (**cited**) it is a convex combination $\sum\lambda_\sigma P_\sigma$, so $\Phi_{E(Q)}(O)=\sum_\sigma\lambda_\sigma(PE(Q))_\sigma\geq0$. $\square$

## 4. Step 2: the support form

**Proposition 4.1 (proved here).** Suppose that for every $x\in X$, every $\pi_*$ and every $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$,

$$Pc_{\pi_*}(x)\geq0\ \Longrightarrow\ \Phi_{c_{\pi_*}(x)}(O)\geq0.\tag{S}$$

Then the theorem holds.

*Proof.* Fix $a,b$ and $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$, and put $x_0=\Psi(v(a,b))$ and $D=\det(D_a+OD_bO^t)$. For $\theta\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ the point $\theta x_0$ lies in $X$ and $F_\sigma(\theta x_0)=\theta z_\sigma$. Choose $\pi_*$ minimizing $\operatorname{Re}(\theta z_\sigma)$. Then $Pc(\theta x_0)\geq0$, and (S) with 3.7 gives $\operatorname{Re}(\theta D)\geq\min_\sigma\operatorname{Re}(\theta z_\sigma)$. This holds for every $\theta$. If $D$ were outside the compact convex set $\operatorname{conv}\{z_\sigma\}$, a strictly separating line would give a $\theta$ violating it. So $D$ lies in the hull, and Corollary 2.2 passes from $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ to $\mathrm U(4)$. $\square$

## 5. Step 3: the contact reduction

**Lemma 5.1 (proved here).** If the affine line $x+\mathbb C\kappa$ lies in $X$ and $Pc(x)\geq0$, then $\Phi_{c(x)}(O)\geq0$ for all $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

*Proof.* By 3.4, $0=\nabla C(x+t\kappa)=\nabla C(x)+tH_\kappa x$ for all $t$, so $H_\kappa x=0$. $H_\kappa$ is real and $H_\kappa\kappa=0$, so $H_\kappa c(x)=0$ and $c(x)\in E\cap V_\mathbb R$. Apply 3.8. $\square$

**Lemma 5.2 (maximum principle; proved here, with classical potential theory cited).** Let $\Sigma$ be a compact connected Riemann surface, $S\subset\Sigma$ finite, and $H_1,\ldots,H_N$ meromorphic functions on $\Sigma$, not all constant, with poles in $S$. On $\Sigma^\circ=\Sigma\setminus S$ put $h_j=\operatorname{Re}H_j$ and $L=\sum_j\lambda_jh_j$ with real $\lambda_j$. Suppose $L\geq0$ at every point of $\Sigma^\circ$ where all $h_j\geq0$ and some $h_j=0$. Let $\Omega$ be a connected component of $\{h_j>0\ \text{for all }j,\ L<0\}$ such that $-L$ is bounded on $\Omega$ near each point of $S$. Then $\Omega$ is empty.

*Proof.* Let $v=-L$ on $\Omega$ and $v=0$ on $\Sigma^\circ\setminus\Omega$. At a boundary point $q$ of $\Omega$ in $\Sigma^\circ$, continuity gives $h_j(q)\geq0$ and $L(q)\leq0$. If all $h_j(q)>0$ and $L(q)<0$, then $q$ has a connected neighbourhood inside the open set $\{h>0,L<0\}$ that meets $\Omega$, so $q\in\Omega$, which is open: impossible. If some $h_j(q)=0$ the hypothesis gives $L(q)\geq0$. So $L=0$ on the boundary. Hence $v$ is continuous, nonnegative, harmonic where positive, and therefore subharmonic on $\Sigma^\circ$. It is bounded above near $S$, so it extends to a subharmonic function on $\Sigma$ (removable singularities), which is constant (maximum principle on a compact surface). If $\Omega\neq\emptyset$ the constant is positive, so $\Omega=\Sigma^\circ$ and every $h_j$ is positive on $\Sigma^\circ$. A function whose real part is positive near an isolated singularity has no pole there. So every $H_j$ is holomorphic on $\Sigma$, hence constant: a contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Zheng, Theorem 3.6; proved here).** Fix $O_0\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$ and $\pi_*$. If some $x\in X$ has $Pc(x)\geq0$ and $\Phi_{c(x)}(O_0)<0$, then some $x'\in X$ has the same two properties and at least six contact permutations.

*Proof.* Not all gaps vanish, since otherwise $c(x)=0$. Scale $x$ by a positive real so that $\sum_\sigma\operatorname{Re}g_\sigma(x)=1$. Suppose $x$ has exactly $k$ contact permutations, $1\leq k\leq5$. Inside $X$ impose the $k$ affine-linear conditions

$$g_\sigma(y)=g_\sigma(x)\ \ (\sigma\ \text{a contact},\ \sigma\neq\pi_*),\qquad\sum_\sigma g_\sigma(y)=\sum_\sigma g_\sigma(x).$$

Since $X$ is irreducible of dimension 6, every irreducible component $Y$ of this set through $x$ has dimension at least $6-k\geq1$ (affine dimension theorem, **cited**).

If all $g_\sigma$ are constant on $Y$, then $Y\subset x+\mathbb C\kappa$ by 3.6, so $Y$ is that whole line, and Lemma 5.1 gives $\Phi_{c(x)}(O_0)\geq0$: a contradiction. So some $g_\sigma$ is not constant on $Y$, and there is an irreducible affine curve $\Gamma\subset Y$ through $x$ on which it is not constant.

On $\Gamma$ the gaps of the old contacts are identically 0 and the sum of all gaps is identically 1. Let $H_1,\ldots,H_N$ be the remaining complex differences. They are not all constant. The fixed number $L=\Phi_{c}(O_0)$ is a real linear combination of their real parts. Let $\Sigma$ be the smooth completion of the normalization of $\Gamma$ (**cited**) and $S$ its points at infinity. At the point of $\Sigma^\circ$ over $x$, all $h_j>0$ and $L<0$. On the component $\Omega$ through it each $h_j$ lies in $(0,1)$, so $L$ is bounded. By Lemma 5.2 the hypothesis of that lemma must fail: there is a point of $\Sigma^\circ$ where all $h_j\geq0$, some $h_j=0$ and $L<0$. Its image $x_1\in\Gamma\subset X$ has $Pc(x_1)\geq0$, $\Phi_{c(x_1)}(O_0)<0$, the same normalization, and at least $k+1$ contacts. Repeat at most five times. $\square$

The point $x'$ is nonzero, so by 3.3 it comes from finite spectra. Throughout, $O_0$ is fixed and only the spectra and the factor $\alpha$ move.

## 6. Step 4: the finite classification

**6.1 Minor vectors.** For $I,J\subset[4]$ with $|I|=|J|\in\{1,2\}$ let $n_{I,J}\in V_\mathbb R$ be the vector with $(Pn_{I,J})_\sigma=1$ if $\sigma(I)=J$ and 0 otherwise. It exists and is unique (EXACT). There are 16 for $|I|=1$ and 18 for $|I|=2$, since $n_{I,J}=n_{I^c,J^c}$.

**Lemma 6.2 (proved here).** $\Phi_{n_{I,J}}(O)=\det(O[I,J])^2\geq0$ for $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

*Proof.* $\Phi_{n_{I,J}}(O)=\sum_{\sigma(I)=J}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\prod_iO_{i,\sigma(i)}=\varepsilon\det O[I,J]\det O[I^c,J^c]$ with $\varepsilon=(-1)^{\sum I+\sum J}$, by Laplace expansion along the rows $I$. Jacobi's complementary minor identity for $O^{-1}=O^t$, $\det O=1$, gives $\det O[I^c,J^c]=\varepsilon\det O[I,J]$. $\square$

**6.3 The other facet vectors.** For $\pi\in S_4$ and $\varepsilon=\pm1$ put

$$t_\pi=\sum_in_{\{i\},\{\pi(i)\}},\qquad d_\pi=n_{12,\{\pi1,\pi3\}}+n_{12,\{\pi2,\pi4\}}-n_{13,\{\pi1,\pi2\}}-n_{13,\{\pi3,\pi4\}},\qquad h_{\pi,\varepsilon}=\tfrac12(t_\pi+\varepsilon d_\pi).$$

These 48 vectors take the values 0 (thirteen times), 1 (ten times) and 2 (once) on the 24 permutations. Their Leibniz sums can be negative: for $O_*=\tfrac17\begin{pmatrix}1&4&4&4\\-4&1&4&-4\\-4&-4&1&4\\-4&4&-4&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\Phi_{h_{\mathrm{id},-1}}(O_*)=-158/2401$ (EXACT). The 82 vectors are numbered $f_0,\ldots,f_{81}$ as in the paper's Appendix D; the library reproduces that table.

**Proposition 6.4 (Kovačec–Ribeiro; Zheng, Proposition 4.1; audit, EXACT).** The polytope $Q=\operatorname{conv}\{v_\sigma\}$, where $v_\sigma(c)=(Pc)_\sigma$, has dimension 13 and exactly 82 facets, the zero sets of the 34 minor vectors and the 48 vectors $h_{\pi,\varepsilon}$. Equivalently, the cone $\{c\in V_\mathbb R:Pc\geq0\}$ has exactly these 82 extreme rays.

Verified in [`reduction/finite-classification.md`](reduction/finite-classification.md) by three independent exact methods: a double-description enumeration, an exhaustive pass over all 2,496,144 thirteen-element vertex subsets, and ridge closure of the facet graph. The paper's own certificate (6,846 simplices whose boundary cancels) also replays.

**Lemma 6.5 (Zheng, Lemma 4.2; proved here, given 6.4).** For a set $T$ of permutations let $N_T=\{c:Pc\geq0,\ (Pc)_\pi=0\ (\pi\in T)\}$. Then $N_T$ is the set of nonnegative combinations of the $f_j$ that vanish on $T$.

*Proof.* By 6.4 and Farkas' lemma every $c$ with $Pc\geq0$ is $\lambda_0\kappa+\sum_j\lambda_jf_j$ with all $\lambda\geq0$. Evaluate at the barycentre of the vertices in $T$: every term is $\geq0$ and the sum is 0. So $\lambda_0=0$ and $\lambda_j=0$ unless $f_j$ vanishes on $T$. $\square$

**6.6 Symmetry (audit, EXACT).** The group $G=(S_4\times S_4)\rtimes C_2$ of order 1152 acts on permutations by $\sigma\mapsto\rho\sigma\rho'$ and $\sigma\mapsto\rho\sigma^{-1}\rho'$. It acts on $V_\mathbb C$ by integer matrices that preserve $X$ and $\kappa$, permute the rows of $P$, and permute the 82 facet vectors, mapping minor vectors to minor vectors. For each $g$ and $O$ there is $O'\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$, a row and column permutation of $O$ or of $O^t$ with one column sign changed if needed, such that $\Phi_{g\cdot c}(O')=\Phi_c(O)$.

**6.7 Orbits (audit, EXACT).** The 134,596 six-element subsets of $S_4$ form 214 orbits under $G$. In 124 of them every $f_j$ vanishing on the set is a minor vector. The other 90 orbits are represented by the sets $T_1,\ldots,T_{90}$ of the paper's Appendix E, one for each orbit. They are listed in Section 9.

## 7. Tools used by the cases

**7.1 Coincident eigenvalues (paper, Appendix B; audit).** [Report](foundations/appendix-b.md): the three statements hold as written.

- *Theorem B.1.* If $a_i+b_j=0$ for some $i,j$, then for every $U\in\mathrm U(4)$ there are permutation weights $\mu_\sigma\geq0$ with sum 1, depending only on $U$, such that $\det(D_a+UD_bU^*)=\sum_\sigma\mu_\sigma z_\sigma$ for all values of the remaining eigenvalues. In particular the theorem holds for such spectra.
- *Lemma B.2.* The same conclusion when two eigenvalues of one matrix coincide.
- *Corollary B.3.* On a curve of spectra normalized by a denominator in which exactly one coincidence factor vanishes at a puncture, the negative part of the Leibniz sum stays bounded near that puncture, for a fixed $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$. It does not cover two factors vanishing at once: each case that needs that gives its own argument.

**7.2 One spectrum on a circle or line (paper, Theorem C.1).** If one of the two spectra lies on a circle or a line, the theorem holds. [Part 1](foundations/appendix-c-part1.md) · [part 2](foundations/appendix-c-part2.md) · [sketched and cited steps](foundations/appendix-c-open-items.md). Two referees found no error. Proposition C.4, sketched in the paper, has a complete proof supplied by the audit. After that work Theorem C.1 rests on Theorem 2.1, on one rank statement at stable fixed points that follows from degree-one generation (Howard–Millson–Snowden–Vakil, **cited**), on projective biduality in characteristic 0 (**cited**), and on the facts of 3.3. Another owner reaches the same conclusion by a different route ([`../unitary-factorization/`](../unitary-factorization/README.md), Theorem P4 and Corollary P4′; not yet independently reviewed). This theorem is needed in full only by $T_7$, $T_{11}$, $T_{24}$ and one auxiliary seven-contact statement. Elsewhere it is used for repeated eigenvalues, where Lemma B.2 suffices.

**7.3 Radial necessity (proved here).** Let $x=u+iv\in X$ with $u,v\in V_\mathbb R$. Then $C(u)=0$ and $\operatorname{Hess}C(u)\,v=0$. In particular $\det\operatorname{Hess}C(u)=0$.

*Proof.* $\nabla C$ is quadratic, so $0=\nabla C(u+iv)=\nabla C(u)-\nabla C(v)+i\operatorname{Hess}C(u)v$. The imaginary part gives $\operatorname{Hess}C(u)v=0$, the real part $\nabla C(u)=\nabla C(v)$. By Euler's formula $3C(u)=u^t\nabla C(u)=u^t\nabla C(v)=\tfrac12v^t\operatorname{Hess}C(u)v=0$. If $v=0$ then $u\in X$ and $\operatorname{Hess}C(u)u=2\nabla C(u)=0$, so the Hessian is singular in every case with $u\neq0$. $\square$

Here $u=c(x)+\operatorname{Re}F_{\pi_*}(x)\,\kappa$. The cases of groups IV and V write $c(x)$ in the generators of its contact cone, substitute into these two equations and eliminate the coefficient of $\kappa$.

**7.4 Hessian rank and inertia (paper, Corollary C.5 and Proposition C.6; audit).** For $x\in X$ the real symmetric matrix $\operatorname{Hess}C(\operatorname{Re}x)$ has rank at most 12 and at most six positive and six negative eigenvalues. [Report](foundations/appendix-c-part1.md); Corollary C.5 has an own proof in the audit. Used by $T_{49}$ and, through auxiliary statements, by $T_{32}$, $T_{33}$, $T_{51}$, $T_{53}$, $T_{79}$, $T_{89}$, $T_{90}$.

## 8. How a case is proved

Fix $\nu$. The statement to prove is

> **Case $T_\nu$.** If $x\in X$ and $c(x)\in N_{T_\nu}$, then $\Phi_{c(x)}(O)\geq0$ for every $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

By Lemma 6.5, $c(x)$ is a nonnegative combination of the $f_j$ vanishing on $T_\nu$. If no non-minor $f_j$ occurs with a positive coefficient, Lemma 6.2 finishes. The proofs differ in how they deal with the non-minor coefficients. The paper sorts them into six groups by the decisive step.

| Group | Decisive step | Cases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | A real cross-ratio puts six eigenvalues on one circle and adds contacts; the larger contact cone has only minor generators. | 1 |
| II | Identities among the eigenvalues and nonnegativity of the gaps give the inequalities needed for a decomposition into minor vectors. | 5 |
| III | Inequalities between coefficients on a real parameter region make a Gram matrix positive semidefinite, which is an inequality of 7.6. | 7 |
| IV | Multiplicative relations among the $z_\sigma$ force the common real part of the contact products to vanish; then the cubic of 7.3 is strictly positive, contradicting $C(u)=0$. | 3 |
| V | The two equations of 7.3, written in the generators, exclude the region where every inequality of 7.6 fails. The exclusion is a finite list of polynomial sign certificates. | 21 |
| VI | A curve in $X$ on which the contact differences are fixed; the negative part of the Leibniz sum is bounded by 7.6; Lemma 5.2 adds a contact, and the larger contact set is an earlier case. The model is the paper's Section 5 for $T_{85}$. | 53 |

The cases cite each other. The paper's Appendix G arranges them, together with 27 statements for seven-element contact sets and four branch statements, in twelve stages so that each proof uses only earlier ones. The audit's [dependency map](deps/dependency-map.md) confirms that this order is acyclic and that all ten conditional theorems of the supplement are discharged by documents that do not depend on their users. ## 10. Step 5: assembly

**Proposition 10.1 (Zheng, Proposition 4.4; proved here, given Sections 6 and 9).** Let $x\in X$ with $Pc(x)\geq0$ and at least six contact permutations. Then $\Phi_{c(x)}(O)\geq0$ for every $O\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$.

*Proof.* Choose six contact permutations; they form a set $T$ with $c(x)\in N_T$. By 6.7 some $g\in G$ maps $T$ either to a set of one of the 124 orbits or to some $T_\nu$. By 6.6, $g\cdot x\in X$, $g\cdot c(x)$ is the gap vector of $g\cdot x$ and lies in $N_{g\cdot T}$, and $\Phi_{c(x)}(O)=\Phi_{g\cdot c(x)}(O')$ for a suitable $O'\in\mathrm{SO}(4)$. In the first case Lemmas 6.5 and 6.2 give $\Phi\geq0$. In the second, Case $T_\nu$ does. $\square$

**Proof of the theorem, given the ninety cases.** By Proposition 4.1 it suffices to prove (S). If (S) failed at some $x$, $\pi_*$, $O_0$, Theorem 5.3 would give $x'\in X$ with $Pc(x')\geq0$, at least six contacts and $\Phi_{c(x')}(O_0)<0$, contradicting Proposition 10.1. $\square$
